# Editorial Standard

Read this before reconnaissance and conception. Its purpose is to find the strongest short book hidden inside a subject, not to inflate a subject into an encyclopedia.

## Interpret the commission

Treat the input as a subject, not automatically the title. Infer the subject type, likely nonspecialist baseline, genuine surprises, necessary prerequisites, evidence types, misconceptions, paradoxes, and obvious overbreadth. Preserve natural-language constraints and the commission's language.

Reconnaissance should reveal chronology, actors, mechanisms, disputes, primary evidence, transformations, myths, geographic variation, technical dependencies, and present-day survivals as the subject requires. Do not decide the table of contents before the terrain is visible.

## Find the book

Generate several plausible conceptions internally. Do not expose a menu unless asked. Compare them by:

1. **Understanding:** will the reader gain a durable mental model?
2. **Importance:** does the scope include what truly matters?
3. **Interest:** does the material provide movement, consequence, character, surprise, or explanatory pleasure?
4. **Coherence:** do the parts belong to one book?
5. **Movement:** does later material become possible because earlier material exists?

Reject category-by-category completeness when a causal, chronological, investigative, conceptual, biographical, geographic, systemic, or hybrid spine can create cumulative understanding. The generic ladder `Origins / Early Development / Turning Point / Modern Era / Legacy` is not a default; use it only when the evidence genuinely demands that exact structure.

## Define the spine and transformation

State internally: `By the end of this book, the reader should understand ______.` Use it as the inclusion test. Choose a spine that makes each chapter feel like the natural continuation of the last: chronology, cause and consequence, development of an idea, an investigation, movement through a system, increasing scale, problem and attempted solution, recurring comparison, or a subject-specific hybrid.

Do not force an argumentative thesis when a narrative or explanatory spine is more honest. Every book needs cumulative coherence, not necessarily a polemic.

## Scope, exclusion, and length

Narrow enormous topics rather than compressing an encyclopedia. State where the book begins and ends, which dimensions are central, which are context, and which attractive tangents are excluded. Choose 10,000–30,000 words only after conception. Write the shortest book that feels complete; the range is a boundary, not a quota.

Interesting research does not authorize scope drift. Test every addition against the reader transformation, approved conception, chapter job, and effect on proportion.

## Chapter architecture

For every proposed chapter, answer:

- What job does it perform?
- What question drives it?
- What changes between beginning and end?
- Why is it here?
- What new understanding becomes possible afterward?

Merge chapters with duplicate jobs. Vary narrative, explanatory, portrait, controversy, walkthrough, and consequence modes when the material benefits, while preserving one voice and spine. Do not prescribe a chapter count.

## Editorial brief and approval

The brief is a fast decision document, not a research report or chapter synopsis collection. Include proposed title/subtitle, one-paragraph conception, scope and exclusions, the explicit factual cutoff for any current or evolving subject, organizing principle, natural length estimate, ordered chapter titles, a short rationale, and **Proceed?**

Approval means “this is substantially the book I want.” It freezes the core commission, not every outline detail. After approval, autonomously improve titles, boundaries, order, emphasis, anecdotes, and length while remaining faithful to the approved conception. Ask a second substantive question only if new evidence makes a reality-supported book fundamentally different from what was approved and the user's core intent cannot be preserved.
